One decision, three layers

Every time it is our turn, the bot runs this whole path. Layers 1 and 2 work side by side. Layer 3 puts their answers together and picks one action.

This is the full design. The live bot today runs the base and a simpler layer 2 (one tally page per opponent, no personalities and no picker). The full layer 2 and the picker are being added to it one tested piece at a time.

  • Base. A run-out means dealing the rest of the cards many times and counting how often we win. The matchup table records how every starting hand fares against every other. The base compares our chance of winning with the price to call, and that gives the base move.
  • Behaviour. Each opponent gets its own notebook page. The page blends five personalities by weight (refit on real tournament hands) and never settles on one fixed label. On real hands most of the value comes from the opponent's own tallies; the personalities help mainly in its first few dozen hands. From that blend it predicts how likely the opponent is to fold, call or raise if we bet a given amount, and what cards it probably holds.
  • Picker and skills. The playbook has five pages. Value shove: go all-in with a strong hand. Bluff: bet a weak hand so they fold. Bet every street: the plan to get all the chips in by the river. Call the shover: call an opponent who goes all-in. Let the barreller bet: let an opponent who keeps betting carry on. Random picks happen only between near-ties.

How each part is trained

All lasting learning happens on our laptops. The tournament starts a fresh bot every round, so the bot can't learn between rounds by itself. A lesson reaches the table only when we upload a new version.

Same-deals test. Two versions of the bot play identical cards, so luck is the same for both. We keep a change only if it wins by more than its error bar, the margin that luck alone could explain.

Fitted offline. Layer 2 is set up on our laptops before it plays: first on simulated bots, where every card is visible, then on real tournament hands. It is judged on rounds it never saw, by how well it predicts opponents' next actions.

What's live right now

v1-fast, also called Joca v4. It is the base plus a simpler version of layer 2.

The first version, v1, used up to 10.03 s of its 10 s time bank. Once it ran out completely and was forced to check or fold 41 times. v1-fast does the same thinking about 3× faster.

A separate thing: the Adaption prize

This is a language model (a chat-style AI) fine-tuned on our bot's decisions, each paired with a written reason. It is our entry for the “Best Use of Adaption” prize.

It can't play in the tournament. The tournament sandbox allows 10 ms per decision, 200 KB and no internet, and this model is far too big and slow for that.

Words we use

Board
The shared cards in the middle of the table.
Pot
The chips bet so far in this hand.
Price to call
How many chips we must add to stay in the hand.
Flop and river
The flop is the first three shared cards. The river is the last one.
Street
One round of betting within a hand.
Run-out
Dealing the rest of the cards to see who would win.
Showdown
The end of a hand, when the players left show their cards.
Same-deals test
Two versions play identical cards, so luck is the same for both.
Error bar
How much a result could move by luck alone.
Neural network
A program that learns patterns from examples.
GPU
A graphics chip that is very fast at this kind of maths.
Clone of a real team
A copycat bot built from the hands that team played.
Time bank
The 10 s of thinking time the bot gets for each 1,000-hand round.
Data token
The team's key for downloading hands from the site.
Lane D
Evaluation and quality: checking each version before it goes out.
Standard library
The tools built into Python, with nothing extra installed.
Bucket tables
Sunday's tables from 09:00 to 11:55, before the final table at 12:05.
Sandbox
The locked-down tournament computer the bot runs on.
